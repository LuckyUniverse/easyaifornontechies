The free ChatGPT course

Six lessons, about ten minutes each, and the first three are free on the site. You don't watch a video. You paste the lesson into ChatGPT and it becomes a tutor that gives you one step at a time, on your own real work, and waits for your answer before moving on. Everything works on the free plan.

The six lessons

Ordered the way OpenAI's own beginner series is ordered, because their view counts show what people actually want: the “ask better questions” lesson pulls more than five times the traffic of their introduction. People have met ChatGPT. They want to stop getting rubbish out of it.

  1. Ask your first question — one real, finished email. Free below, right now.
  2. Ask better questions — the four things that turn a bland answer into a good one. The lesson that fixes most people's frustration. Free, on its own page.
  3. Turn a mess into a plan — one thing you're dreading, converted into a checklist you'll use. Free, on its own page.

Two of the six are about safety. That is deliberate, and it matches the ratio in OpenAI's own beginner course.

Lesson 1: Ask your first question

What you'll have at the end: an email you actually needed to send, written in something close to your own voice. About ten minutes.

Why most people's first try fails

Most people open ChatGPT, type something like “write me an email”, get back something that sounds like a press release, and quietly close the tab.

That isn't you failing. It's the same as walking up to a stranger and saying “help.” Helpful people need to know what you're doing and what a good result looks like. So we're not going to start with theory. We're going to write one real email — one you actually owe somebody — and you'll see the difference in about eight minutes.

Copy this into ChatGPT

Copy everything in the box and paste it into a new ChatGPT conversation. It turns ChatGPT into a tutor for this one lesson: it gives you a step, waits while you do it, then asks what happened. 

You are my patient tutor. I am NOT technical. Teach me by having me
do one thing at a time, not by explaining.

Rules for this whole conversation:
- Give me ONE step. Then STOP and ask what happened. Wait for my answer.
- Never show me step 2 before I have done step 1.
- No jargon. If a technical word is unavoidable, explain it in one
  sentence with an everyday comparison.
- If I say "it didn't work", do not move on. Ask me what I saw, and
  fix it.
- Keep every message under 150 words.
- Re-read these rules before every message you send me.

Lesson: write a real email that sounds like me.

Step 1 is to ask me who I actually owe an email to right now, and what
it needs to say. Ask me that now, and nothing else.

What should happen

  1. It asks who the email is for. Give it a real one — someone you're genuinely putting off. A made-up example teaches you nothing.
  2. It asks you to paste an email you've written before. Any one. It doesn't have to be good — it's listening for how you write, not judging it.
  3. It writes a draft, then stops and asks: does this sound like you, or too formal?
  4. You tell it what's off. It fixes that one thing without starting over.
  5. It names what you just did: you gave it the situation, an example of your voice, and feedback.
Step 3 is the lesson. Everything before it sets up that moment and everything after names it. When the draft comes back sounding like you, you'll understand in one second what a paragraph of explanation could never teach you.

If it runs ahead of you

Paste this and it will get back in line:

Go back to being the tutor. One step at a time. I was on step 3.

If you get stuck

  • “I don't have an old email to paste.” Tell it how you talk instead — warm, brief, formal? That works nearly as well.
  • “I don't owe anyone an email.” Use a thank-you note, or a reply you've been putting off.
  • “It just wrote the whole thing immediately.” That's the drift problem. Paste the line above.

You're done when you have an email you'd actually send, and you can say why the second version was better than the first.

Questions

Is the ChatGPT course really free?

Yes. Lessons one, two and three are on the site in full, with nothing to sign up for. The last three come in the starter kit by email. Everything works on ChatGPT's free plan.

Do I need to pay for ChatGPT to do these lessons?

No. Every lesson in the course is built to work on the free plan. If a paid plan is ever genuinely worth it for something, we say so plainly and explain what it buys you.

How long does each lesson take?

About ten minutes. Each one does a single thing and then stops, and you finish it with something real rather than notes.

What makes these different from a video course?

You are not watching someone else's screen. You paste the lesson into ChatGPT and it becomes your tutor: it gives you one step, waits while you do it on your own work, and asks what happened before moving on. "It didn't work" is always an accepted answer.

What if ChatGPT stops following the lesson?

Paste this: "Go back to being the tutor. One step at a time. I was on step 3." Longer conversations drift, and every lesson ships with that line for exactly this reason.

Do I need to install anything?

No. Everything happens in your web browser, the same place you check email. Nothing to download and nothing to set up.
